## A Simple Question with a Difficult Reverse Direction

Start with modular exponentiation. We choose a base, raise it to a power, and keep the remainder after division by a modulus.

For example,

$$
5^6\equiv8\pmod{23}.
$$

If we know the exponent is $6$, we can compute the result. Now reverse the question:

> Given the base $5$, the modulus $23$, and the result $8$, can we recover the exponent?

We want to solve

$$
5^x\equiv8\pmod{23}.
$$

This is an instance of the **discrete logarithm problem**.

The example is small enough to solve by trying powers. With appropriately chosen large groups, recovering the exponent can be much more difficult than computing the power. That gap motivates cryptographic constructions based on discrete logarithms.

More generally, in a finite cyclic group generated by $g$, the problem is to find $x$ such that

$$
g^x=h.
$$

“Cyclic” means that every group element can be reached by taking powers of $g$. If the group has order $n$, those powers repeat every $n$ steps. The exponent is therefore determined **modulo $n$**, rather than as one unique integer among all integers.

## Pohlig–Hellman: Let the Factorization Do the Work

Pohlig–Hellman uses the factorization of the group order to break the discrete logarithm problem into smaller pieces.

Suppose

$$
n=q_1^{e_1}q_2^{e_2}\cdots q_r^{e_r},
$$

where the $q_i$ are distinct primes. Instead of recovering the entire exponent immediately, the method recovers

$$
x\pmod{q_1^{e_1}},\quad
x\pmod{q_2^{e_2}},\quad\ldots,\quad
x\pmod{q_r^{e_r}}.
$$

For a prime-power factor $m_i=q_i^{e_i}$, define

$$
g_i=g^{n/m_i},\qquad h_i=h^{n/m_i}.
$$

The original equation implies $g_i^x=h_i$. Since $g_i$ has order $m_i$, this smaller problem determines $x$ modulo $m_i$.

The **Chinese Remainder Theorem** then combines these residues into one exponent modulo $n$.

One distinction matters: the smaller problems take place in **subgroups of prime-power order**. We do not simply replace the original arithmetic modulus with each prime-power factor.

### A Small Example

Return to $5^x\equiv8\pmod{23}$. The nonzero residues modulo $23$ form a group of order $22$, generated by $5$, and

$$
22=2\cdot11.
$$

For the order-$2$ part, raise both sides to the eleventh power:

$$
(5^{11})^x\equiv8^{11}\pmod{23}.
$$

Since $5^{11}\equiv-1$ and $8^{11}\equiv1$, we learn that $x$ is even:

$$
x\equiv0\pmod2.
$$

For the order-$11$ part, square both sides:

$$
(5^2)^x\equiv8^2\pmod{23},
$$

so $2^x\equiv18\pmod{23}$. In this small example, checking powers gives $2^6\equiv18$, hence

$$
x\equiv6\pmod{11}.
$$

Combining the two pieces gives

$$
x\equiv6\pmod{22}.
$$

The example shows the central idea: **recover information about the exponent in smaller groups, then reconstruct it.**

## Why This Matters for Cryptography

Pohlig–Hellman shows why the size of a group is only part of the story. The factorization of its order matters too.

When the order factors entirely into small primes, the method can make the discrete logarithm problem much easier. Having a small factor alone does not mean the whole problem is easy: a large prime-order component can remain difficult.

This helps explain the role of large prime-order subgroups in cryptographic design. Studying algorithms that solve discrete logarithms helps us understand which mathematical settings are suitable for relying on their difficulty.

That connection is what made Pohlig–Hellman an appealing subject for a project combining number theory and formal verification.

## What Coq Adds to the Picture

An informal proof might say, “The subgroup solutions agree with the reconstructed exponent, so the original equation follows.” A formal development has to specify the objects and assumptions supporting that conclusion.

Coq lets us express mathematical definitions and propositions, then construct proofs that its kernel checks. The relevant question becomes:

> Under the stated assumptions, does the conclusion follow through the permitted logical steps?

Our paper organizes the reasoning around an abstract group structure, prime-power subproblems, and reconstruction by the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

### Reading a Small Piece of the Coq Code

The code becomes easier to follow when we put it next to the mathematics. These are excerpts from the paper, meant to be read within its surrounding definitions and assumptions rather than compiled as a standalone program.

First, the development declares a solver for the smaller problems:

```coq
Parameter discrete_log_primepower :
  G -> G -> nat -> nat -> nat.
```

Read the arrows from left to right. The solver takes two group elements, a prime, and an exponent, then returns a natural number. In a call such as `discrete_log_primepower g_i h_i p k`, the intended task is to find the logarithm of `h_i` to the base `g_i` in a subgroup of order $p^k$.

The word `Parameter` matters. It introduces a component without implementing it. The paper separately assumes a correctness specification for this solver. Declaring its input and output types alone does not establish that it returns the right answer.

### Building the Smaller Problems

The next function builds the list of exponent residues:

```coq
Fixpoint build_rems (facs : list (nat * nat)) (h : G) : list nat :=
  match facs with
  | [] => []
  | (p, k) :: rest =>
      let m := p ^ k in
      let g_i := pow gen (order / m) in
      let h_i := pow h (order / m) in
      discrete_log_primepower g_i h_i p k
      :: build_rems rest h
  end.
```

There is a lot of notation here, but the function follows the same steps as the mathematical description:

- `facs` is a list of pairs. A pair `(p, k)` represents a prime-power factor $p^k$ of the group order.
- `Fixpoint` introduces a recursive function. Here, each call processes one factor and continues with the remaining list.
- `[] => []` is the stopping case: no factors remain, so there are no more residues to compute.
- `let m := p ^ k in` gives the current prime-power factor the name `m`.
- `pow gen (order / m)` and `pow h (order / m)` form the projected base and target for the smaller problem.
- `::` places the solver's answer at the front of the list returned by the recursive call.

The two lines defining `g_i` and `h_i` are the code version of

$$
g_i=g^{n/m},\qquad h_i=h^{n/m}.
$$

For our example modulo $23$, the factors are $2$ and $11$. With the intended subgroup solver, `build_rems` produces the residues $0$ and $6$. Those are the two pieces of information we used to reconstruct the exponent.

Notice that `order / m` uses natural-number division. The mathematical setup must ensure that $m$ divides the group order. This is one of the details that a formal proof has to justify explicitly.

### Putting the Pieces Back Together

Once the residues are available, the main definition is short:

```coq
Definition discrete_log (h : G) : nat :=
  let n := order in
  let facs := factor n in
  let mods := map (fun pk => (fst pk) ^ (snd pk)) facs in
  let rems := build_rems facs h in
  CRT_solve mods rems.
```

`Definition` gives a name to this computation. The input `h` is the group element whose logarithm we want, and the output has type `nat`, the natural numbers.

The line defining `mods` converts factor pairs into prime powers. Here, `fst` takes the first entry of a pair, `snd` takes the second, and `map` applies the same function to every entry in a list. For example, the pair `(2, 3)` becomes $2^3=8$.

The final call, `CRT_solve mods rems`, combines the remainders using the Chinese Remainder Theorem. In our small example, it combines moduli $2$ and $11$ with remainders $0$ and $6$, giving the representative $6$ modulo $22$.

This is the part I find especially satisfying: the main definition follows the mathematical plan closely. Factor the order, solve the smaller problems, and combine their answers.

### Where the Proof Enters

Writing the computation is only part of the work. We still need to connect its output to the equation we wanted to solve.

One step in the paper's proof introduces an element measuring the difference between the reconstructed power and the target:

```coq
set (D := op (pow gen x) (inv h)).
```

In this line, `op` is the group operation, `pow` is exponentiation, and `inv` is inversion. In mathematical notation, it says

$$
D=g^x h^{-1}.
$$

If we can show that $D=e$, where $e$ is the identity, then multiplying by $h$ gives $g^x=h$.

The proof strategy is to use the subgroup equations to show that this difference disappears in every prime-power component. Under the required cyclic-group and order assumptions, those components together force $D$ to be the identity.

This is how the code and proof fit together. The definitions describe how the candidate exponent is assembled; the proof must explain why that candidate solves the original equation.

## The Scope of the Formalization

An important part of describing this work is being clear about its assumptions.

The paper treats foundational number-theoretic results, including factorization properties and the Chinese Remainder Theorem, as trusted assumptions. It also treats the prime-power discrete logarithm solver as a black box with an assumed correctness specification.

The development therefore focuses on the **decomposition and reconstruction strategy under those assumptions**. It does not provide an independently verified implementation of every component, nor does it prove the security of a complete cryptographic system.

There is more work to do in making the foundations self-contained: proving the supporting results, implementing and verifying the subgroup solver, and tightening its specification to require the target to belong to the subgroup generated by its base. That last condition matters because a logarithm to a given base need not exist outside its generated subgroup.

For me, this is an important connection between logic and cryptography: a correctness claim is only as meaningful as the statement and assumptions being checked.
